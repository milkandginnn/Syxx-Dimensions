Classification First: FMEA as a Cognitive Diagnostic System in
STEM
locating the failure mode in reasoning, an approach drawn from reliability engineering

Robert Antonio Ramiarina, M.Sc.
Reta Razão, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil. Independent researcher.
(M.Sc. in Biomedical Engineering, COPPE/UFRJ; Licensed in Mathematics.)

Abstract
This work proposes a diagnostic system that applies principles from reliability engineering to cognitive error in STEM.
It decomposes problem-solving into discrete reasoning steps, classifies each failure mode against a typified catalog,
and prioritizes intervention according to its actual impact, measured by a Risk Priority Number (RPN = S × O × D).
The approach is a direct transposition of Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA), a well-established reliability
method, to the domain of student reasoning.
The central contribution is classification itself. Before any scoring or intervention, classifying an error converts it from
an ephemeral event into structured, countable, and comparable data. This single act generates the diagnosis, produces
a map of the failure landscape, and reveals that weaknesses that appeared unrelated often stem from a single underlying
mechanism. The RPN does more than rank failures: its factorization prescribes the most effective intervention in each
case, whether to prevent occurrence, improve detection, or reduce severity. The catalog organizes failure modes into
layers of cognitive complexity, while the continuous qualification of empirical data closes the learning loop. This
qualification grounds occurrence in observed data, turns the RPN into an evolving trajectory, and enables the catalog
to refine itself. The framework is presented as the formalization of a diagnostic practice developed and refined over
more than a decade, within a two-decade trajectory extending from biomedical reliability engineering to educational
diagnostics.

1. Introduction
Remediation in STEM still operates like pre-diagnostic
medicine: it identifies that a student is struggling and
simply prescribes more of the same content, without
ever locating the precise point of failure. Faced with
poor performance, the teacher repeats material: the
aggregate symptom is treated without isolating the
mechanism of the error, the exact link in the chain of
reasoning where the student stops producing the correct
step. The determining deficit, often a single link of low
visibility, remains untouched, hidden behind correct
answers that simulate mastery. The problem is not the
amount of remediation, but the absence of precise
localization.
Reliability engineering has solved a structurally
analogous problem for decades. With finite mitigation
resources, effort must be directed to the failures that
combine high severity, high frequency, and low
detectability, and its canonical instrument for this is
FMEA (Failure Mode and Effects Analysis). This work

This adaptation is the most recent stage of a continuous
trajectory that runs from reliability in health care to
education. It originates in the modeling of hospital cost
from patient characteristics [1, 5] and in the
quantification of risk by diagnostic classification,
notably the Charlson Comorbidity Index adapted to
ICD-10 [2, 3, 4]. The conceptual axis, to quantify risk
from a structured decomposition of states in order to
prioritize intervention, is exactly the one that reappears,
now transposed, in educational FMEA.
That earlier experience carries a lesson that guides all
that follows: ICD-10 treats no one. Its value is to make
morbidity codable, countable, comparable, and
communicable, and it is on that classification that the
Charlson Index is built. This primacy of classification,
and not the priority number that follows from it, is what
constitutes the foundational gain of cognitive FMEA
(Section 3).
Yet the antecedent goes beyond a mere precedent of
classification. The study that adapts the Charlson Index

2. The FMEA framework applied to
cognition
Industrial FMEA [6, 7] decomposes a system into
functions and, for each one, enumerates failure modes,
effects, and causes, scoring each mode by severity (S),
occurrence (O), and detectability (D). The transposition
keeps the architecture and redefines the objects: the
"system" is the procedure for solving a type of problem;
the "functions" are the reasoning steps; a "failure mode"
is the typified way in which a step fails to be executed,
not the generic error but the named, catalogable one.
• Severity (S): how far the failure compromises the
result.
• Occurrence (O): how often the student commits
the failure mode, frequently conditional on the
context that triggers it, estimated by systematic
diagnosis.
• Detectability (D): how hard the failure is to
identify. This is an inverted axis: the more the
failure goes unnoticed, or is misdiagnosed by
conventional instruments, the higher the score.
Each axis is scored on an ordinal scale from 1 to 10,
anchored by descriptors, and it is this anchoring that
makes the scoring reproducible across raters rather than
a free judgment. For severity, low values correspond to
failures that alter only a recoverable detail of the result;
intermediate values, to failures that change the answer
but in a way still correctable downstream; high values,
to failures that invert or invalidate the answer.
Occurrence is anchored in the observed frequency of the
mode, and detectability in the fraction of times the
failure escapes the conventional instrument.

The final product orders the failure modes by
remediation priority. The result is not a global judgment
about the student, but an ordered map of specific targets.

3. Classification as the primary gain
Before any score or intervention, there is an act that
produces value on its own: classifying the error,
assigning it to a typified failure mode in the catalog. The
previous preprint treated this act as a step in the pipeline.
It is more than that: it is the ground on which everything
else rests, and it returns value the instant it happens.
The central argument is direct: classification converts
the error from an event into data. An unclassified error
is consumed the moment it occurs; it may be corrected
ad hoc, but it leaves no structured information behind. A
classified error becomes an instance of a named type,
and therefore countable, comparable, communicable,
and accumulable. Every later analysis depends entirely
on this conversion: estimating occurrence, computing
the RPN, choosing the intervention lever, and tracking
improvement. Without classification there is no data;
without data there is no FMEA. Classification is not a
step toward value; it is the substrate of all value.
Aerospace reliability engineering has operated under
this same primacy for decades. In Fault Tree Analysis (a
method created in 1962, during the development of the
Minuteman missile), the structured enumeration and
naming of each failure event precedes any quantification
of probability, and the qualitative tree itself, still without
numbers, already constitutes a relevant engineering
deliverable [11].
Four gains follow from classification, all independent of
any remediation.
The diagnostic space becomes bounded. Without a
catalog, diagnosis is open inference, with countless
possible explanations for the same error. Classifying
against a finite, typified catalog turns diagnosis into
recognition within a known set of possibilities. This is
what makes the process tractable and reproducible, and
it is also the structural basis of inter-rater agreement
(Section 4): because the catalog fixes the vocabulary and
anchors the criteria to a defined set, rather than leaving
them to each rater's free judgment, two professionals
who apply the same catalog to the same data tend to
reach the same classification.
Classification produces the collapse "one mode, not
three." The recognition that three manifestations in
three disciplines are instances of a single failure mode
(Section 7) is a result of classification, not of
intervention. The moment that recognition happens, the

Classification gives the student a nameable target. To

demoralizing. To say "you carry a specific mechanism,
you process the stimulus halfway, and it surfaces here,
here, and here" offers a single, identifiable, attackable
target. This change in the student's relationship to the
error is a real gain that costs neither RPN nor complex
intervention: it costs only the act of naming.
Classification resolves the error on two axes: mode
and topic. The mode axis identifies the cognitive
mechanism behind the error; the topic axis locates,
under canonical codes, the specific content where the
failure occurred. The cross-tabulation of the two (mode
× topic) is the system's full diagnostic resolution.
Without the topic axis the failure cannot be located;
without the mode axis the mechanism cannot be
generalized. It is exactly the crossing of the two that
distinguishes "the student is weak in this content" from
"the student carries this cognitive mechanism, which
happens to surface in this content," and that is the
distinction the framework exists to make. The fully
completed classification sheet becomes, in itself, a
classified inventory of failure modes: a deliverable
relevant to the decision before any decision, just as the
industrial FMEA sheet is a result independent of the
mitigations that derive from it.

4. Inputs, outputs, and the diagnostic loop
The system has well-defined operating boundaries. The
input is the set of the student's responses to diagnostic
instruments, that is, questions designed, from the
failure-mode catalog itself, to elicit and discriminate
specific reasoning steps. These instruments are defined
by their diagnostic content, not by their channel:
applying them in writing or in an oral session does not
change their validity. The output is an ordered map of
the failure modes present, each with its RPN, and the
priority remediation target that follows from it.
Between input and output, the cycle is: decompose the
resolution into steps, classify the failure mode of each
step against the catalog, score S, O, and D, order by
RPN, and direct remediation to the top of the order.
What makes the structure a system, rather than a
sequence of isolated judgments, is that this cycle closes
on the catalog. The same catalog serves three coupled
functions: it designs the diagnostic instruments (each
question is built to elicit a step and discriminate its
failure mode), classifies the observed responses, and
supplies the axis against which occurrence is
accumulated over time. The full circuit is therefore:
catalog, instrument, captured response, classification,
empirical occurrence, RPN, directed remediation, new
captured response, feeding back the occurrence estimate
(Figure 1). This closure is the subject of Section 8.

5. RPN, the three levers, and what the
factorization prescribes
RPN = S × O × D

(1)

Modes with a high RPN receive mitigation first. But the
RPN is more than a priority number: its factorization
says what kind of intervention to apply. The RPN can be
reduced along three distinct paths, each corresponding
to a mode of action.
Reduce O (prevent). Make the failure occur less often.
This is classic remediation: eliminate the cause of the
failure mode through directed teaching. It suits
eliminable modes, typically conceptual ones.
Reduce D (detect). See the failure when it occurs. This
is the principle that distinguishes this work: an
instrument designed to isolate a failure mode changes
the D score. A failure that was invisible, or
misdiagnosed (high D), becomes detected and
unambiguously attributed (low D), and the RPN drops
by construction, before any intervention on the student.
This drop becomes a direct measure of the instrument's
value: it need not fix the student, only make visible and

correctly attributable what was invisible. In the terms of
classic FMEA, this instrument is the method's detection
control, and improving it is, literally, a recommended
RPN-reduction action, which confirms that the
transposition is faithful to the method, not a loose
analogy. Detection-as-control is the central axis of the
diagnostic, and it holds primacy among the three levers,
because it is also the act that carries out the classification
of Section 3.
Two senses of "risk" are worth distinguishing here.
Lowering D through the instrument reduces the mode's
priority for the tutor, because the failure, once invisible,
becomes locatable and interceptable; but it does not, by
itself, reduce the probability that the student will err on
the exam. That probability falls only when intervention,
on O or on S, follows detection. Detection enables
directed action; it does not replace it.
A good detection control is measured along two faces.
The first is sensitivity, that is, capturing the failure mode
when it is present, which is what the reduction of D
above describes. The second is specificity, that is, not
firing when the mode is absent, correctly returning a true
negative. An instrument that flagged the mode also in

someone who does not carry it would have poor

Reduce S (contain). The failure still fires, but it is
contained before it reaches the final result. This is the
cognitive analogue of graceful degradation: teaching the
student to catch the slip before it compromises the
answer. Through self-checking, rereading protocols, or
metacognitive checklists, the mode keeps occurring (O
unchanged), but the final answer comes out correct. It is
especially useful for modes resistant to elimination,
typically the low-visibility slips that no student
eliminates entirely.
The factorization of the RPN therefore prescribes the
following: when the RPN is dominated by D, the
problem is visibility, and the response is to build the
instrument; when dominated by O, the problem is
prevalence, and the response is to remedy the mode;
when dominated by S, the problem is consequence, and
the response is to teach the student to contain the slip.
This rule is standard practice in reliability engineering;
the contribution here is to transpose it to the educational
context, giving the teacher a clear, principled criterion
for deciding what kind of intervention to apply, instead
of always falling back on reinforcing content.
A note on rigor: whether self-checking reduces S or O
depends on where one draws the boundary between
mode and effect. If we define the mode as "committing
the slip" and the effect as "wrong answer," self-checking
breaks the link between mode and effect, which is a
reduction of S. If, instead, we define the mode as "an
uncaught slip that reaches the final answer," selfchecking reduces O. The choice of this boundary is an
explicit modeling decision, and the framework forces
the researcher to make it consciously, an obligation that
is a source of precision, not of ambiguity.
Finally, the RPN admits two further uses. As an
allocator of a finite resource: given the scarcity of
tutoring time, the ordering by RPN indicates where it is
worth investing, which is the metric's original purpose
in industry. And as a longitudinal trajectory: the RPN
profile across diagnostic cycles becomes a quantitative
measure of progress, made possible by the systematic
qualification of the data (Section 8).

6. The layers of cognitive complexity
The catalog is not a flat list of failure modes; it organizes
them into layers of cognitive complexity, from the
processing of input information to integration and

evaluation. Each layer groups modes of its own nature
and, decisively, requires its own detection instruments:
detecting an input failure is not the same problem as
detecting an evaluation failure.
• Input layer: registering and fully processing the
stimulus. Failures here are failures of capture, since
decisive elements of the prompt are ignored before
reasoning even begins.
• Integration layer: relating the captured elements,
translating between representations, and applying
relations within their domain of validity. Failures
here are failures of processing, since the material
was read but combined or applied incorrectly.
• Evaluation layer: judging the plausibility of the
result, verifying, and criticizing one's own product.
Failures here are failures of closure, since an absurd
result is accepted for lack of verification.
This layered view of cognitive failure has a direct
precedent in the reliability literature on human error.
Reason's distinction between slips and lapses (failures of
execution and attention) and mistakes (failures of
intention and judgment) already separates error by
cognitive stage [12]; the slip, in particular, is the inputlayer failure whose containment (the S lever)
corresponds to what Reason calls error recovery. That
the reliability theory of human error maps onto the
layers reinforces that importing reliability engineering
into cognition is not a metaphor.
The integration layer deserves particular note, because it
houses a failure mode well documented in mathematicseducation research: the failure of conversion between
registers of semiotic representation [8]. In Duval's
theory, a mathematical object is not directly accessible,
since it exists for the student only through signs. To
reason mathematically is therefore to carry out
transformations within a single register (treatment) and,
above all, between distinct registers (conversion): from
the verbal to the algebraic, from the algebraic to the
graphical, from the graphical to the tabular. Conversion
is the cognitively costly point, and its failure (solving the
equation correctly but being unable to read the
corresponding graph, or failing to translate the prompt
into a model) is a failure mode distinct from undue
generalization (Section 7.2), though both belong to the
same layer. This failure has a dual anchorage in the
literature: what Duval theorizes as conversion, the
approach of Lesh and colleagues treats on the
instructional plane, under the concept of translation
between representations (real situations, concrete
models, figures, language, and symbols) [10]. The two
lines converge on the point this framework exploits:

Under FMEA, this is not three distinct problems but a
single failure mode with a high RPN. The baseline is S
= 8, O = 7, and D = 9, the last of these high precisely
because the failure disguises itself as a weakness
specific to each subject, or as mere inattention, and
escapes conventional diagnosis; the RPN is therefore
504.

The catalog of modes is complemented by a typology of
incorrect responses (distractors), treated in separate
work, which classifies the observable errors into
families and subtypes under formal Latin nomenclature:
12 families and 41 subtypes. One example is the family
Ampliatio, which gathers the errors of undue extension
of a rule's scope, precisely the mechanism that the undue
generalization of Section 7.2 instantiates. Mode, layer,
and distractor together form the diagnostic vocabulary
against which each error is classified.

The first lever applied is detection. A diagnostic
instrument designed to separate command of the concept
from processing of the prompt reduces D from 9 to 3,
and does so before any contact with the student. The
RPN falls to 8 × 7 × 3 = 168, a value that directly
measures the diagnostic gain.

The layer also informs the likely lever (Section 5): input
failures, resistant to elimination, tend to call for
containment (S); integration failures, being conceptual,
tend to call for prevention (O); evaluation failures tend
to call for installing habits of verification. The link is not
deterministic, but it guides the choice, and it is what the
following illustration makes concrete.

7. Illustration: two modes, two layers, two
levers
Consider two failure modes from distinct layers. The
scores come from real diagnoses in practice,
anonymized, on an ordinal scale from 1 to 10.

7.1 Input layer: incomplete processing of the
stimulus (S lever)
The student processes only part of the information
presented and ignores elements decisive for the answer,
typically a single word that carries, on its own, the
information that settles the question and that is not fully
extracted. It is a single failure mode that, by manifesting
differently in each discipline, is usually diagnosed as
three separate weaknesses. In physics, it is inattention to
the weight of a term that silently fixes a condition (in
free fall, "maximum height" imposes zero velocity at the
highest point), and the student continues the calculation
with a v that the prompt had already nullified, even with
the concept fully understood. In reading comprehension,
it is the omission of a negation or a qualifier that changes
the direction of the prompt's meaning, even reversing it
at the limit. In economics, it is swapping "marginal"
(cost, revenue, or utility) for the total or the average,
even while mastering marginal analysis.

The intervention recognizes that this failure mode resists
elimination, since no student stops skipping a word now
and then. The high-impact lever is therefore not the
reduction of occurrence (O) but the containment of
severity (S), through a self-checking protocol that
contains the slip. With S reduced from 8 to 3, the final
RPN is 3 × 7 × 3 = 63.
The fall of the RPN from 504 to 63 shows the gain in
precision that comes from treating the failure mode as a
single entity with specific levers: by correctly
identifying that severity, not occurrence, was the
highest-impact lever, the problem moves from high
priority to controlled risk with modest resources.

7.2 Integration layer: undue generalization (O
lever)
The student applies a valid relation outside its domain of
validity. In mathematics, the student linearizes the
nonlinear: (a+b)2 = a2 + b2, √(a+b) = √a + √b, log(a+b)
= log a + log b. In physics or chemistry, the student
treats a gas as ideal where the approximation does not
hold, or applies relations of uniform motion to
accelerated motion. Where the conventional instructor
reads these errors as isolated gaps in content ("doesn't
know special products," "doesn't know radicals,"
"doesn't understand gases") and prescribes three
separate reinforcements, FMEA identifies a single
failure mode of unambiguous mechanism: extending a
rule beyond its domain of validity.
The baseline is S = 9 (the failure produces a confident
error), O = 6, and D = 8 (read as distinct content gaps);
the RPN is therefore 432. The detection instrument
reduces D from 8 to 3, and the RPN falls to 9 × 6 × 3 =
162. The intervention then exploits that this mode is
eliminable: by teaching the student to state explicitly the
domain of validity of a rule and to check whether the
instance fits it before applying it, occurrence falls from

8. Continuous improvement through data
qualification
Classifying converts error into data (Section 3);
capturing and classifying systematically, over time,
converts data into qualified data, typified by mode,
located by topic, situated by layer. This qualification is
what closes the loop of Section 4 and enables what no
point-in-time assessment reaches.
Occurrence becomes empirical. The frequency of each
failure mode stops being a desk estimate and becomes
derived from accumulated real responses. The O of the
RPN gains a measurable origin, what distinguishes
cognitive FMEA from a sophisticated rubric.

progressively increasing their discriminating power.
The classification the instruments produce feeds back
into the design of the instruments that follow.
This continuous qualification is also what turns the
value hierarchy into a living system rather than a static
scheme. At order zero is classification (the map). At the
first order is the RPN (the route through the map, under
a resource constraint). At the second order are the
intervention levers (the vehicle). The three levels are
nested and non-redundant, and each turn of the loop
refines all three (Figure 2).

9. The methodology as consolidated
practice

The catalog becomes self-refining. When an error fits
no existing mode, this is a sign that the catalog is
incomplete. The classification gap itself comes to guide
the creation of new modes and the revision of existing
criteria. Data qualification thus does not only feed the
catalog; it audits it continuously.

The framework is not a desk proposal. It is the
formalization of a diagnostic practice developed and
applied continuously over more than a decade, with
students preparing for university-entrance exams
(vestibular), the SAT, IB curricula, and international
exams. Three pillars sustain it: a versioned canonical
corpus of dated definitions, templates, and classification
criteria that stabilizes application across cases; the
catalog of failure modes organized by layers of
cognitive complexity (Section 6); and a data substrate
that systematically captures and qualifies the students'
responses (Section 8).

Instrument design becomes evidence-driven. By
identifying which modes show a high RPN and in which
topics they most surface, it becomes possible to design
the next instruments with precise focus on those modes,

10. Automating classification at scale
With the advance of today's language models, the
preliminary classification of failure modes from written
responses can be automated at scale. This is no longer a
speculative possibility: assigning an error to a mode
within a finite, typified catalog is exactly the kind of
classification-against-a-defined-taxonomy task these
models perform reliably. And it is the classification-first
architecture itself (Section 3) that makes this automation
viable, since a structured catalog exists to classify
against and the model operates on a well-posed target,
unlike the open, unbounded diagnosis of "what is wrong
with this student."
What remains with the specialist is not a reserve of
caution but a structural division of labor: the calibration
of severity (the axis that most demands judgment among
S, O, and D), the diagnostic closure, and the governance
of the catalog. Automation covers the classification; the
specialist decides what it means, clinically and
pedagogically, and what to do about it. The substrate of
qualified data (Section 8) also serves as the training and

calibration material that makes the automatic
classification progressively more precise, closing the
improvement loop at this level too.

11. Contribution
The contribution of this work is not a metaphor but an
operational protocol: it takes educational diagnosis, still
done today by global impression, and converts it into a
localization of the error that is at once precise and
prioritizable. This protocol is organized in three nested
levels, and the force of the argument is that each level
rests on the one before.
At the foundational level is classification. It converts the
error from an ephemeral event into data, and from this
single act come the diagnosis, a classified inventory of
failure modes, and the recognition that symptoms
scattered across several disciplines are in fact
manifestations of a single mechanism. Classification
operates on two axes at once: the mode axis, which
identifies the cognitive mechanism, and the topic axis,
which locates the content; and it is the crossing of the
two that gives the diagnosis its resolution.
On this base rises the second level, the risk priority
number. Its factorization into severity, occurrence, and
detectability does more than order the failures by
urgency: it indicates the most effective action in each
case. The third level, that of the intervention levers, is
what this indication sets in motion: preventing
occurrence, improving detection, or reducing severity.
And here is the most original point of the framework:
when detection comes to function as a control, the very
fall of the risk it produces directly measures the value of
the diagnostic instrument.
Sustaining the three levels is the substrate that closes the
loop: a catalog organized in layers of cognitive
complexity and the continuous qualification of the data.
This qualification is what makes occurrence, the
trajectory of progress, and the catalog itself empirical
and capable of refining themselves. None of this is
offered as an isolated theoretical exercise: it is the
formalization of a diagnostic practice consolidated over
more than a decade, within a two-decade trajectory that
runs from biomedical reliability to educational
diagnostics.

12. Limits and directions for future work
The formal quantitative validation follows two steps.
The first, and more tractable, is inter-rater reliability: the
agreement between two independent raters who apply
the same catalog to the same data, measurable by a
coefficient such as Cohen's or Fleiss's κ. This is the
validation that the classification-first architecture
(Sections 3 and 4) implicitly promises, and the natural
next step. The second is predictive accuracy against
independent educational outcomes; while the conceptual
and methodological framework for this validation is
already consolidated in both the reliability lineage and
the biomedical antecedent, its specific application to
cognitive FMEA has not yet been carried out.
The scale of severity, occurrence, and detectability is
ordinal and calibrated empirically to practice, following
the tradition of industrial FMEA itself; its formal
standardization across cohorts is a direction for
refinement. The system does not use the RPN as a final
metric measure but as an internal prioritization
mechanism that feeds a reading by bands and prescribed

actions, aligning with the current movement in the field,
which has shifted to Action Priority tables.
The detailed characterization of the failure modes,
including the taxonomy by layers of cognitive
complexity and the typology of distractors, is the subject
of complementary work in the same program.
