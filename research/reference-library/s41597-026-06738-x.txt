In the context of Industry 4.0, Industrial Control Systems (ICSs) are undergoing a significant transition
from physical isolation to partial openness. While this shift enhances operational efficiency and
integration, it also exposes ICSs to increasing network attacks. Effective intrusion detection methods
rely on high-quality ICS datasets, yet existing options remain limited in scope, diversity, and realism.
To address this gap, we introduce ICS-NAD, a dataset collected in real-world ICS scenarios with three
well-known ICS brands. It contains two attack traffic sample patterns and covers 20 common ICS
attack types. Through feature extraction and labeling, the ICS-NAD dataset provides 60 features with
complete labels. We validate its utility using 10 machine learning and deep learning classification
models. The dataset comprises 245.96 GB data files, including raw ICS network traffic (in PCAP format)
and extracted features with labels (in CSV format). It is publicly available on the website to support ICS
network attack detection research in academic and engineering contexts.

Background & Summary

Industry 4.0 advancements are driving traditionally air-gapped Industrial Control Systems (ICSs) toward
increased connectivity. This shift, while essential for enabling smart manufacturing and maintenance through
technologies like AI, big data analytics, and digital twins1,2, unavoidably exposes inherent ICS vulnerabilities
to malicious attackers. Consequently, ICSs have emerged as a prime target for escalating cyberattacks3, facing
ever-growing threats and damage. This trend is underscored by many serious ICS attack incidents worldwide in
recent years4–6, causing significant economic and social losses.
To effectively address this challenge, the availability of suitable ICS datasets is vital. Many critical applications
designed to defend against ICS network attacks, e.g., AI-driven anomaly detection, signature-based analysis, and
behavioral monitoring, heavily rely on such datasets for both training robust detection models and rigorously
evaluating their performance, thereby accurately identifying ICS network threats within real-world operational
environment7.

Related work. However, existing ICS datasets suffer from three primary limitations, restricting their wide
usage. We categorize 28 start-of-the-art datasets into three groups based on the limitations. (L1) Datasets with
small testbeds or simulated network traffic. (L2) Datasets with non-ICS-specific or limited attacks. (L3) Datasets
with insufficient features or imperfect labels. We list them in Table 1 based on the network scenarios and publication years of these datasets.
Datasets with small testbeds or simulated network traffic. The ISCX dataset8 has virtual network traffic created
by agents that follow predefined network states. The New Gas Pipeline dataset and the Water Storage dataset9 are gathered from two laboratory-scale ICSs and include three attacks. The Battle of the Attack Detection
Algorithms (BATADAL) dataset10 only has simulated records and cannot reflect real network traffic. On the
website of iTrust11, three pertinent datasets are available, i.e., the Secure Water Treatment (SWaT) dataset, the
Water Distribution (WaDi) dataset, and the Electric Power and Intelligent Control (EPIC) dataset. Their testbeds
are well-organized but small. The ICS-flow dataset12 includes four types of network attacks and an open-source

Datasets with non-ICS-specific or limited attacks. The DARPA dataset14 and the KDD Cup’99 dataset15
have 41 features and include four traditional network attacks. The CAIDA dataset and the MAWI dataset 16
are widely used, but their attacks are not ICS-specific. The CTU-13 dataset17 is designed for botnet attacks,
and the DDoS2016 dataset18 focuses on DDoS attacks. Both of them contain only one type of attack. The
UNSW-NB15 dataset19 is proposed to reflect modern network traffic, and it only contains limited attack types.
The Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity (CIC) provides the CICFlowMeter tool to extract rich features. Three
known studies employ it for dataset processing, namely the CICDoS dataset20, the CICIDS2017 dataset21, and
the CICDDoS2019 dataset22. The WUSTL-IIoT dataset23, the TON-IoT dataset24, and the Edge-IIoTSet dataset25 extract alerts and system resources as features. However, the above six datasets are not targeted at ICS. To
sum up, most of these datasets are not specifically designed for ICS, and traffic with non-ICS-specific attacks is
inapplicable in the ICS network.
Datasets with insufficient features or imperfect labels. The Kyoto 2006 dataset26 involves various attacks collected through honeypots, but it is not labeled. The Twente dataset27 is the first publicly available labeled dataset for flow-based intrusion detection, but it lacks packet-based features. The UGR’16 dataset28 is suitable for
long-term network analysis, but problems with mislabeling require further examination29. The CIDDS-001 dataset30 is a flow-based dataset, and lacks packet-based features. The Water Distribution Testbed (WDT) dataset31
involves two hours of records, but its network dataset only has limited 14 packet-based features. The X-IIoTID
dataset32 contains 18 types of attacks, exceeding other datasets in variety. However, missing data confines its
wide usage12. The Electra dataset33 is well-designed with three types of ICS attacks, and all the records are from
realistic devices like PLCs. However, only Modbus-related features, e.g., function code and error, are provided.

reflect network characteristics from two different aspects. Therefore, the defect of any one of them will affect the
overall dataset utilization effect12.

Our dataset.

To address the aforementioned three limitations, we present the ICS-NAD dataset34 as a
benchmark for ICS network attack detection datasets. In particular, (1) Large-scale real-world dataset collection environment. The ICS-NAD dataset34 is based on a large ICS test site, which has 10 ICS brands and hundreds of Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs), as well as 10 sets of industrial processes in different fields, e.g.,
thermal power generation and sewage treatment. In producing the ICS-NAD dataset34, we choose three sets of
ICSs from three well-known brands, each utilizing a different network protocol. The reason for this selection is
that their protocols and packets are not encrypted, which provide more information for the dataset users. (2)
ICS-specific attacks. We carefully select 20 common ICS network attacks and group them into four categories:
Reconnaissance, Denial of Service (DoS) and Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS), False Data Injection (FDI),
and Man-in-the-Middle (MitM). (3) Two attack sample patterns (ASPs). In existing datasets, the traditional ASP
consists of normal traffic followed by continuous one-time attack traffic. Except for this, the ICS-NAD dataset34
also provides a more practical and consistent pattern, i.e., normal traffic and randomly happened attack traffic
appear 19 times in turn. Specifically, each cycle contains three minutes of traffic with 30-second attack bursts
occurring randomly within the second minute. This ASP reflects more detailed traffic characteristics, including
the process where the traffic becomes abnormal gradually after the attack begins, and the process where the network gradually recovers after the attack is over, which provides dataset users with additional information of the
ICS traffic. (4) Rich features and complete labels. The ICS-NAD dataset34 is not anonymized and contains 60 features extracted through ICSFlowGenerator12, including both flow-based and packet-based features. We enhance
labeling efficiency through a two-step strategy and clearly label all the records as either benign or the name of the
attack, enabling convenient usage for researchers in the ICS network attack detection field.

Methods

This section details the collection and processing methodology for the ICS-NAD dataset34. First, we describe
the real-world testbed environment, including ICS scenarios, network topology, and the selection of 20 representative ICS network attacks. Next, we outline the attack execution process of the two ASPs. We then present
the methods for capturing and storing the raw network traffic. Finally, we cover the dataset processing steps, i.e.,
feature extraction and labeling of the ICS-NAD dataset34. The complete processing procedure is shown in Fig. 1.

Data collection. Dataset collection environment. Our dataset collection environment contains three sets of

ICSs from three well-known ICS brands and two sets of industrial processes, which can reflect multiple real-world
ICS scenarios. The dataset collection environment can be divided into the physical domain and the network
domain.
As shown in Fig. 2, the physical domain has two comprehensive ICS scenarios, i.e., thermal power and sewage plant. The former scenario has complete thermal power generation facilities, e.g., reheaters, burners, and
steam turbines, while the latter scenario contains eight tanks for processes including sedimentation and filtration of impurities.
Three brands of ICSs are used to control these two industrial processes. Configuration information is shown
in Table 2. To be concrete, the thermal power employs ABB’s ICSs as its controller and uses the private TCP
protocol for network communication. The sewage plant can utilize either Siemens’s ICSs or Schneider’s ICSs to
control the industrial processes. Siemens’s ICSs use the S7Comm protocol, and Schneider’s ICSs use the Modbus
protocol. Three ICSs are independent of each other in terms of wiring so that dataset records do not clutter.

As for the network domain, all the ICSs share the same network topology, as illustrated in Fig. 3, because
of similar device numbers and network scales. Our topology employs two human-machine interfaces (HMIs)
to send operation commands and corresponding PLCs to control the industrial processes. HMIs and PLCs are
connected through one switch. We employ a server as an attacker to eavesdrop or inject attack traffic into the
network. This access of the attacker server can be obtained using various methods, e.g., malicious software,
backdoors, and remote access12. There is another server that captures and stores the traffic data. Table 3 provides
comprehensive details for each device.
ICS-specific attacks. ICS network is at risk mainly due to the absence of authentication mechanisms, data
encryption, and integrity checks33, which allows attackers to conduct network attacks based on eavesdropping,
traffic flooding, and packet modification12. Therefore, we conduct the following ICS attack types, which have
been identified as common yet critical in previous researches33–35 and reports from the European Union Agency
for Cybersecurity36.
1. Reconnaissance attacks. They surreptitiously collect information about target victims, e.g., IP addresses,
operating systems (OSs), and open ports. In our experiment, we use the Nmap tool to conduct IP scans and
port scans.
2. DoS and DDoS attacks. The DoS and DDoS attacks send a large number of packets in a short period,
resulting in a denial of service. We use the Hping3 tool to generate DoS and DDoS traffic. Moreover, we
choose two packet-sending speeds in the optional parameters, i.e., fast and flood, to reflect different attack
intensities. In the fast mode, ten packets are delivered per second. In the flood mode, Hping3 uses the fastest speed of the network interface card to generate attack traffic.
3. FDI attacks. They use false data, false responses, and false commands33 to fool victims. We use the Netwox
tool to generate four FDI attacks. For the large ICMP packet attack, we send ICMP packets that are too
large to deal with in the ICS devices. For the ICMP redirection attack and the ICMP packet unreachable
attack, we change the route message of packets to trick the network routing. We also generate packets with
wrong TCP flags to disrupt the protocol parsing process.

Attack traffic sample patterns. In the ICS-NAD dataset34, we generate two types of attack patterns in the
attacker server to reflect rich traffic characteristics in different attack scenarios, as shown in Fig. 4. The first type
is typically seen in existing datasets and lasts for five minutes. In the first minute, we have only regular network
traffic, while an attack is executed in the following four minutes. This ASP can reflect the degree and severity of
the attack through continuous attack traffic. The second type is designed for DoS and DDoS attacks and contains
20 cycles of three minutes each, for a total of one hour. The reason for this design is that these attacks utilize a
large amount of traffic to cause service denial, and the process of flow variation contains rich information7. The
first cycle only includes regular packets. During the remaining 19 cycles, an attack occurs randomly and lasts
for 30 seconds in the second minute of each period. Therefore, in the second ASP, the first and the third minutes can display the initiation and recovery processes of the DoS and DDoS attacks, which are vital for attack
pre-detection.
Dataset collection and storage. When collecting the ICS-NAD dataset34, we utilize the following steps. First, we
set the workflow of the switch in our topology and let it forward all the traffic in the ICS network to the storage
server. Then, this server uses the TCPdump tool to capture all the raw ICS traffic and store it in the PCAP format.
In addition, we run a script on the attacker server to automatically generate attack traffic in the ICS network with
two different ASPs. During this process, the attacker server also stores logs that contain the timestamps at which
attacks happen, which are utilized to help the labeling process.

Data processing. Feature extraction. Datasets containing only flow-based features or packet-based features cannot fully reflect network status. Flow-based features show the characteristics of flows, e.g., packet numbers and latency between packets. Packet-based features contain the information inside the packets, e.g., TCP
flags and protocols. Therefore, we consider the ICSFlowGenerator12 as a practical feature extraction tool, because
it can extract both types of features. By modifying the source code, we optimize its work pattern to meet our

Labeling. Labeling can be a challenging task. For one thing, the number of network packets can easily reach
hundreds of millions. If each packet is judged when labeling, even using an automated approach can consume
considerable time. For another, our dataset contains 20 types of ICS network attacks, which makes it difficult to
accurately distinguish benign and attack records using a fixed scheme. Therefore, we propose a two-step strategy
to accomplish this task. First, when generating attacks, we log the timestamps for each attack when it occurs.
Therefore, we can roughly jump to the positions where attack records first appear in the dataset and skip the
benign records, thereby reducing the overall judging time for labeling. Second, we use specific judgment criteria
for each attack to distinguish attack packets from benign packets based on attack characteristics, e.g., protocol,
IP address, and TCP flag. Through the two-step strategy, we clearly and efficiently label all the records in the
dataset as either benign or the name of the attack. Both features and labels are stored in CSV format.

Data Records

The ICS-NAD dataset34 is publicly available in the Science Data Bank repository (https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.25938). A total of 245.96 GB of ICS network records and 272 data files are provided. Our dataset is
divided into eight dictionaries, including raw ICS network traffic stored in PCAP format, extracted features
stored in CSV format with complete labels, and log files of the attack timestamps.
1. ABB, Schneider, and Siemens dictionaries: Contain raw ICS network traffic data (in PCAP format) captured
from ABB, Schneider, and Siemens ICSs, respectively. Each PCAP file includes one minute of normal traffic and four minutes of attack traffic with a specific ICS network attack.
2. ABB-LONG, Schneider-LONG, and Siemens-LONG dictionaries: Contain raw ICS network traffic data (in
PCAP format) captured from ABB, Schneider, and Siemens ICSs, respectively. Each PCAP file includes
20 cycles of ICS traffic. In each cycle, three minutes of ICS traffic is captured, and a 30-second attack burst
occurs randomly within the second minute.
3. Labeled_CSV dictionary: Contains CSV files corresponding to the PCAP files. These CSV files contain 60
features extracted from the raw ICS traffic, encompassing both packet-based and flow-based features. All
the records are clearly labeled as benign or the name of the attack in the last column of the CSV files.
4. Time_of_Attack dictionary: Contains the log files of the timestamps that attacks happen, which are utilized
for the labeling process.

demonstrate real-worldness by analyzing key characteristics of the traffic samples against operational ICS environments. Finally, we validate practical usability through training and evaluating 10 commonly used machine
learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) classification models on the ICS-NAD dataset34.

Real-world ICS traffic characteristics. Since all traffic is captured in real-world ICS scenarios, the ICS-NAD dataset34 accurately reflects intrinsic ICS network characteristics. To provide a quantitative and objective characterization that facilitates cross-dataset comparison and deeper analysis, we employ a set of time-series statistical features.
Taking the Siemens traffic as an example, the data samples of normal traffic and ICMP flood attack traffic are shown
in Figs. 5 and 6. As summarized in Table 6, these features are calculated for both normal traffic and attack traffic,
offering clear insights into how attacks alter network behavior. The time granularity for feature calculation is 100 ms.
1. Skewness. The skewness quantifies the asymmetry in traffic volume distribution. Normal traffic exhibits
positive skewness, i.e., 1.94, indicating a right-skewed distribution where most time intervals have low
packet counts, punctuated by occasional of ICS operational events. Under an ICMP flood attack, the skewness increases to 2.56, confirming that the attack creates a more pronounced asymmetry with high-volume
spikes, which is a signature of malicious flooding activity.
2. Kurtosis. Kurtosis describes the concentration of data around the mean and in the tails. During the ICMP
flood attack, kurtosis rises sharply from 2.10 to 5.32, indicating a transition to a sharply peaked distribution with heavy tails. This feature indicates a significant increase in the peak flow during the ICMP flood
attack process.
3. Approximate entropy. Approximate entropy measures the predictability and regularity of sequential traffic
patterns. Both normal and attack scenarios show irregularity in the real-world ICS environment, due to
heterogeneous device communications. But the attack causes a slight increase in entropy from 1.16 to 1.25,
indicating a reduction in predictability.

4. Zero ratio. The zero ratio directly measures network idle time by calculating the proportion of time bins
with no traffic. Normal traffic shows a high zero ratio of 43.17%, concretely evidencing the low traffic rate

Evaluation of 10 ML and DL models. We test the ICS-NAD dataset34 usability by 10 commonly used ML

and DL classification models: Iterative Dichotomiser 3 (ID3), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Random Forest (RF),
Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT), Support Vector Classifier (SVC), Extreme Learning Machine (ELM),
Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM), Adaptive Boosting (AB), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), and
Artificial Neural Network (ANN). This selection synthesizes models that have been validated in prior ICS dataset studies12,22. To ensure a comprehensive and unbiased assessment, the chosen models span four distinct and
complementary families. (1) Tree and tree-ensemble models: ID3, RF, GBDT, LGBM, and XGB. (2) Distance and
kernel-based models: KNN, and SVC. (3) Neural network models: ANN, and ELM. (4) Adaptive ensemble model:
AdaBoost. This diverse and principled selection allows for a robust evaluation of the dataset across different models.

Data preparation. During the processing of the ICS-NAD dataset34, we conduct the following steps. (1)
Columns where all the values are equal are deleted. (2) Empty places are filled with 0. (3) String type values, e.g.,
address and timestamp, are converted to numeric types. (4) Values are normalized and scaled to a range between
0 and 1. (5) The records are split into two subsets, i.e., the training set (60%) and the testing set (40%).
Feature selection. Our dataset has 60 features, but using all of them for training will reduce efficiency and may
introduce additional noise. Therefore, we employ the Random Forest Regressor class of scikit-learn21 to rank the
importance of each feature. This method has been adopted in benchmark studies for its stability and ability to
identify features that contribute most to the model prediction performance12,22. Following the precedent of the
related works, we select the top four features for model training12,22. This parsimonious subset promotes efficient

Model validation. The core task for each ML and DL model is to predict whether a traffic sample characterized
by the four selected features represents benign traffic or a specific attack type from the ICS-NAD dataset34. For
each ML and DL model, we first need to find its most suitable hyperparameters. Through a series of parameter
tuning experiments33, we get the relatively universal ones for each model. All the datasets are trained with
the same set of hyperparameters to ensure fairness. Since we generate two ASPs to reflect multiple anomalies,
the ICS-NAD dataset34 is not balanced. Thus, we use four classical metrics to reflect the training effect, i.e.,
accuracy, recall, precision, and F1-score. As shown in Table 7, ideal training results can be obtained. Four core
metrics can basically reach more than 90 (full score set to 100). Considering all the results, we believe that SVC,
ELM, and KNN are relatively suitable models for the ICS-NAD dataset34 in our experiment. On the other hand,
we should admit that the recall value for the MitM dataset generally reaches 100, which is related to the small
number of positive samples caused by the characteristics of the attack type.
In summary, we substantiate our dataset’s scale through benchmarking against the ICS-flow dataset12, confirm its real-world representativeness through traffic characteristic analysis, and demonstrate practical usability
through training 10 ML and DL models. Experimental results collectively validate the value of the ICS-NAD
dataset34 for ICS network attack detection research.

Potential research applications.
including but not limited to:

Our dataset supports a variety of analyses in the ICS security domain,

1. Supervised learning for attack detection. The CSV files, with their 60 extracted features and complete
labels, are suitable for training and evaluating supervised ML and DL models. Researchers can benchmark
new detection algorithms, compare model performance, or explore feature importance for ICS-specific
threats.
2. Unsupervised and semi-supervised attack detection. The availability of both normal and attack traffic
allows for the development and testing of attack detection models that do not rely solely on labeled attack
data. This is valuable for identifying novel or zero-day attacks.
3. Protocol analysis and modeling. The raw PCAP files contain traffic for three critical ICS protocols. Researchers can use these to analyze protocol state machines, establish baselines for normal operational behavior, and study how the documented attacks violate protocol specifications or communication patterns.

Advice for access and use.

The dataset is split into PCAP and CSV formats to serve different research
needs. Using tools like Wireshark can access PCAP files for deep packet inspection and flow analysis. The CSV
files are ready for use in data science environments, e.g., Python and MATLAB. The label column clearly distinguishes attack classes from normal traffic.

Data availability

The ICS-NAD dataset34 is publicly available in the Science Data Bank repository (https://doi.org/10.57760/
sciencedb.25938). Totally 245.96 GB of ICS data records and 272 data files are provided. All the files are divided
into eight dictionaries, including raw ICS network traffic stored in PCAP format, extracted features stored in CSV
format with complete labels, and log files of the attack timestamps.

Code availability

Python codes for processing the ICS-NAD dataset34, together with 10 ML and DL models, can be obtained freely
at https://github.com/zxzx2002/Code-for-ICS-NAD-Dataset.git, with no restrictions to access.
